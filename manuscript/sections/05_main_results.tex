\section{Main Results}

\subsection{Can the same design strategy sustain the target operator shape over a finite angular domain?}

The representative test is a freeform metasurface designed for large-NA, high-transmission, broadband second-order Fourier differentiation. This target is deliberately demanding. A successful device must suppress the low-spatial-frequency center, increase its response toward larger transverse wavevectors, and do so over a finite incidence-angle interval rather than at a single nominal point. In the project data, second-order ranking is evaluated wavelength by wavelength, with top candidates selected according to a score that combines center suppression, target-shape agreement, and edge response. The resulting distributions already reveal a meaningful design landscape: across the sampled wavelengths, the valid candidate pool contains a long tail of low-quality structures and a much smaller subset of high-scoring geometries, characteristic of a sparse but nonempty feasible set rather than an artificially easy design problem.

The strongest second-order candidates are physically interesting for two reasons. First, their verified response retains the expected even symmetry and outward growth associated with a second-derivative transfer function. Second, this behavior is not confined to one isolated wavelength in the archive. The ranking summaries and per-wavelength top-candidate plots indicate that the same response-centered design logic remains meaningful across a finite operating window. The target is therefore better understood as a structured operator specification over angle and wavelength, not as a one-point curve-fitting exercise.

\subsection{Do surrogate prediction and verified physics agree on the same response object?}

A critical question for any data-driven inverse-design procedure is whether the learned approximation and the physical solver are evaluating the same optical object in a sufficiently similar way. Here that object is the response field itself. The project makes the distinction explicit: the surrogate first estimates target-conditioned response quality, but the final maps and scores are recomputed after RCWA evaluation. This separation is essential because response-field design can fail in subtle ways. A candidate may preserve the target curvature near the center while distorting the shoulders; it may look promising under a reduced metric while leaking power outside the desired angular support; or it may align with the target only after normalization, masking amplitude defects that remain relevant for device operation.

The verified second-order results show why full response-field comparison is more informative than single-number reporting. The score components used in the repository encode the features that matter physically: low transmission near zero spatial frequency, recovery of the desired second-order envelope, and preservation of useful edge response over the design aperture. Candidates with similar nominal aggregate scores can still differ appreciably in how these contributions are balanced. This is precisely where the response-space viewpoint becomes useful. By comparing the verified field rather than a local performance number, one can distinguish designs that merely resemble the target near one condition from designs that sustain the correct operator geometry across the intended angular span.

The same reasoning explains why post-generation reranking cannot be dispensed with. If surrogate agreement alone were enough, physical and nominal rankings would coincide up to noise. In practice they do not. The project retains separate validation and RCWA-based reranking stages precisely because the inverse map is rich enough that small geometric differences can produce consequential differences in the verified operator field. In this context, physical consistency is not a philosophical preference. It is the operational rule that keeps the final design tied to scattering physics rather than to a learned approximation.

\subsection{Does response-level agreement translate into practical operator behavior?}

Operator metasurfaces are ultimately judged by what they do to optical fields and images, not only by how smooth their score curves appear. For that reason, image-domain tests are included in the project as a secondary but important validation layer. The second-order target is particularly suitable for this purpose because its expected action on an input image is intuitive: broad low-frequency content is suppressed while curvature-sensitive features and edge-like structures are enhanced. When the verified response is used for image-processing demonstrations, the resulting outputs recover the characteristic second-derivative behavior rather than an arbitrary contrast-enhancement artifact.

This translation from response-level agreement to operator-level behavior closes the scientific argument of the section. A strong inverse-design result is not merely a structure that scores well against an internal metric. It is a structure whose RCWA-verified response has the correct operator shape and whose action on representative inputs is consistent with that shape. This representative large-NA, high-transmission, broadband example therefore supports the central thesis of the paper in a precise way: conditioning on the target response field, sampling multiple candidates, and selecting them through physical verification is sufficient to recover a practically meaningful Fourier-operator metasurface without reducing the problem to a manually specialized geometry family.
